# Murderer at the Door

You are hiding a friend in your house. Someone comes to the door who seems intent on jailing and/or killing them. They ask you, directly, whether your friend is inside.

_Would you lie? What would you say?_

Alternatively, suppose the person at the door is a police officer, acting under a law that requires you to hand over the person you are hiding. You still believe they will be jailed and/or killed.

_Does anything change? Does it matter whether you lie outright, refuse to answer, or answer truthfully but incompletely?_

---

Source: Immanuel Kant, ["On a Supposed Right to Lie from Benevolent Motives"](https://bgillette.com/wp-content/uploads/2011/08/KANTsupposedRightToLie.pdf) (1797), [Wikipedia Article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_a_Supposed_Right_to_Tell_Lies_from_Benevolent_Motives). Kant's answer: "Truthfulness in statements that cannot be avoided is the formal duty of man to everyone, however great the disadvantage that may arise therefrom for him or for any other." Kant grants that if you lie, you do the murderer no wrong, but argues a lie "does harm to humanity in general, inasmuch as it vitiates the very source of right." (In short, his answer is "no, one should never lie in this situation.")

Kant is answering [Benjamin Constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Constant) "On Political Reactions," *France*, 1797, who wrote that "the moral principle stating that it is a duty to tell the truth would make any society impossible if that principle were taken singly and unconditionally," and that a German philosopher "goes as far as to assert that it would be a crime to tell a lie to a murderer who asked whether our friend who is being pursued by the murderer had taken refuge in our house." Constant argues that "no one has a right to a truth that harms others."

See also: Helga Varden, ["Kant and Lying to the Murderer at the Door... One More Time: Kant's Legal Philosophy and Lies to Murderers and Nazis,"](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9833.2010.01507.x) *Journal of Social Philosophy* 41, no. 4 (2010): 403-421.

## The "Nazi at the door" version

Although it's not Kant's version, one popular version replaces the murderer with a Nazi officer searching for Jews in hiding, such that the person at the door now has "the law" behind them.

In the [paper](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9833.2010.01507.x) above, Helga Varden writes: "Does Kant really mean to say that people hiding Jews in their homes should have told the truth to the Nazis, and that if they did lie, they became co-responsible for the heinous acts committed against those Jews who, like Anne Frank, were caught anyway?"

There is also a real-world case: Le Chambon-sur-Lignon, a French Protestant village that sheltered and moved thousands of refugees during the German occupation. When an official demanded that pastor André Trocmé produce a list of Jews in the village, Trocmé replied: "We do not know what a Jew is. We know only men." The villagers also forged identity and ration cards, and some never became comfortable with the forging (e.g. Magda Trocmé recalled feeling uncomfortable because "duplicity for any purpose was simply wrong.")

Source: Philip P. Hallie, [*Lest Innocent Blood Be Shed: The Story of the Village of Le Chambon and How Goodness Happened There*](https://archive.org/details/lestinnocentbloo00hall) (Harper & Row, 1979).
