Ten Philosophical Ethical Dilemmas to Explore

1) Euthyphro dilemma
   - Posed by: Plato (in Euthyphro), widely discussed by philosophers of religion
   - Dilemma: Are actions morally good because God commands them, or does God command them because they are good? Either horn leads to problems for divine-command accounts of morality.
   - Sources: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — Divine Command Theory / Euthyphro: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/divine-command/

2) The Trolley Problem and Doctrine of Double Effect
   - Posed by: Philippa Foot (modern formulation) and Judith Jarvis Thomson (variants); relates to earlier debates about killing vs. letting die and the Doctrine of Double Effect
   - Dilemma: When (if ever) is it permissible to sacrifice one person to save several? Many variants (switch, footbridge) force tension between consequentialist and deontological intuitions.
   - Sources: SEP — Moral Dilemmas / Doctrine of Double Effect: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-dilemmas/  https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/double-effect/

3) Nozick's Experience Machine
   - Posed by: Robert Nozick
   - Dilemma: Would you plug into an "experience machine" that guaranteed pleasurable experiences but no contact with reality? The thought-experiment challenges hedonist accounts of well-being.
   - Sources: SEP — Nozick / Hedonism discussions: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nozick/  https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hedonism/

4) The Problem of Moral Luck
   - Posed by: Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel (popularized the modern problem)
   - Dilemma: How can agents be morally judged for actions influenced by factors beyond their control? Moral luck strains standard views that moral responsibility requires control.
   - Sources: SEP — Moral Luck: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-luck/

5) The Repugnant Conclusion and Population Ethics
   - Posed by: Derek Parfit (modern articulation)
   - Dilemma: Certain plausible axioms of population ethics imply that a very large population with lives barely worth living can be better than a smaller very happy population — an apparently repugnant implication.
   - Sources: SEP — Population Ethics: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/population/

6) Prisoner’s Dilemma (moral and rational choice implications)
   - Posed by: Early game-theorists (Axelrod popularized in ethics), philosophers analyze moral lessons
   - Dilemma: Individuals acting rationally can arrive at collectively worse outcomes; raises puzzles about cooperation, trust, and moral motivation.
   - Sources: SEP — Prisoner’s Dilemma: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/prisoners-dilemma/

7) The Dirty Hands Problem
   - Posed by: Michelangelo (historical roots), modernly discussed by authors like Michael Walzer
   - Dilemma: Can political leaders be morally justified in committing wrongdoing (e.g., torture, deception) for morally important outcomes? How to reconcile moral guilt with political necessity?
   - Sources: SEP — Dirty Hands: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/dirty-hands/

8) Hume’s Is-Ought Problem (the Fact-Value Gap)
   - Posed by: David Hume (G. E. Moore later related it to the naturalistic fallacy)
   - Dilemma: How, if at all, can normative conclusions (ought) be derived from purely descriptive premises (is)? This underpins debates about moral foundations.
   - Sources: SEP — Hume’s Moral Philosophy / Is–Ought: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hume-moral/

9) The Non-Identity Problem
   - Posed by: Derek Parfit
   - Dilemma: When actions affect which people will exist in the future, can those actions be wrong if no particular person is harmed (because the alternative is that person never exists)? This challenges standard harm-based ethics.
   - Sources: SEP — Non-Identity Problem: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nonidentity/

10) Supererogation and the Demandingness of Morality
   - Posed by: Debates across utilitarians and critics (e.g., Peter Singer highlights demandingness); historical roots in discussions of supererogation
   - Dilemma: How demanding should morality be? Are there permissible acts that go above duty (supererogatory), and how to balance charity-style demands against ordinary moral life?
   - Sources: SEP — Supererogation / Demandingness: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/supererogation/

Notes and suggested next steps
- These ten items are classic philosophical dilemmas (many original as thought-experiments) and are open-ended rather than simple either/or choices.
- If preferred, each can be expanded into a short vignette, assigned primary texts (Plato, Nozick, Parfit, Nagel/Williams, Thomson/Foot), and paired with secondary SEP entries or critiques.
