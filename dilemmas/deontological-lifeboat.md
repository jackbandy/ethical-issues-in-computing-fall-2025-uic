# The Crowded Lifeboat

Your ship strikes an iceberg and sinks. You are one of the crew in a lifeboat that was lowered in a hurry, along with more passengers than the boat can actually carry. The boat sits low in the water, a leak has opened, and a storm is approaching.

An experienced sailor on the lifeboat confirms that unless the boat is lightened, the boat will sink and everyone in it will likely drown. There is no time to build a raft and no cargo/objects/items left to throw over.

Several options are raised:

(1) Do nothing, and let the boat take on water with everyone still aboard.
(2) Draw lots, so that everyone faces the same odds, and some randomly-chosen people jump ship.
(3) Let the crew choose who goes, on the grounds that they alone can sail the boat.
(4) Something else.

_What should be done? Who can or should decide what to do?_

---

Source: "The Overcrowded Lifeboat," entry 1 in ["Some Moral Dilemmas"](https://friesian.com/valley/dilemmas.htm), The Friesian School.

Real case: [*United States v. Holmes* (1842)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Brown_%28ship%29#Trial). After the ship *William Brown* struck an iceberg off Newfoundland in 1841, the crew of an overloaded and leaking longboat threw more than a dozen male passengers overboard. Alexander Holmes was [tried and convicted](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F.Cas/0026.f.cas/0026.f.cas.0360.pdf) of manslaughter, although none of the other crewmen were ever brought to trial. The court held that if the decision had been made based on a lottery (i.e. drawing lots), it might have been permissible/lawful.

Real case: [*R v. Dudley and Stephens* (1884)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R_v_Dudley_and_Stephens). After the yacht *Mignonette* sank, four survivors drifted for weeks in a small lifeboat. After some time, men killed the cabin boy, and the three remaining men ate him; they were rescued four days later. The court refused to accept necessity as a defense, holding no one may set themselves up as judge of whose life is worth more. Both men were sentenced to death and then pardoned to six months' imprisonment.

See also: the [Tongan castaways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tongan_castaways) (1965–66). Similar to the other stories here, this case involved a shipwreck, no imminent rescue, and uncertainties about survival. In this case six boys, ages 13 to 19, were shipwrecked on an uninhabited island and survived there for fifteen months before being rescued. They divided the work in pairs (garden, kitchen, guard), kept a fire burning for over a year, and even set and healed a broken leg. I learned about this story from Rutger Bregman's book [*Humankind: A Hopeful History*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humankind:_A_Hopeful_History).

See also: A. W. Brian Simpson, *[Cannibalism and the Common Law: The Story of the Tragic Last Voyage of the Mignonette and the Strange Legal Proceedings to Which It Gave Rise](https://archive.org/details/cannibalismcommo0000simp)* (University of Chicago Press, 1984).
