George Saunders once [observed](https://www.themarginalian.org/2026/07/20/george-saunders-uncertainty/) that "any question in the form 'Is X right or wrong?' could benefit from another round of clarifying, story-oriented questions. Question: 'Is X good or bad?' Story: 'For whom? On what day, under what conditions? Might there be some unintended consequences associated with X? Some good hidden in the bad that is X? Some bad hidden in the good that is X? Tell me more.'"

A good way to approach these dilemmas is to begin by describing the dilemma in your own words. Instead of jumping straight to the options, try to explain the scenario with precision and detail. This will often help you grasp the options, discover additional options, and find more grounding to approach the dilemma. The point is not to arrive at a particular choice, but to develop deeper reasoning and understanding.

When discussed in class, these dilemmas also give us an opportunity to notice how others approach ethical dilemmas. For example, students may "rank" some values higher than other values (e.g. generosity, frugality, honesty, hospitality), and these dilemmas often put some values in tension with other values.

Additionally, discussing these values in the context of simulated/emulated/hypoethetical ethical dilemmas can help you prepare for dilemmas you will face in the real world.
